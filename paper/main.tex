\begin{abstract}
Inadequate lubrication accounts for an estimated 30--80\% of rolling element bearing
failures, yet reliable online, non-invasive lubrication condition monitoring (LCM)
remains unsolved.
This paper presents an exploratory study in which acoustic emission (AE) and passive
ultrasound (UL) signals are simultaneously acquired from a ball bearing during a
continuous speed sweep (1--\SI{5985}{rpm}) at temperatures spanning
33--\SI{100}{\celsius}, covering boundary, mixed, and full-film lubrication regimes
across 8\,783 sweep--sensor pairs per channel.
The ISO~281 viscosity ratio $\kappa \in [0.003,\,1.64]$ serves as a continuous,
physics-grounded regression target.
LightGBM on AE features alone achieves the best hold-out performance
($R^2 = 0.951$, $\text{RMSE} = 0.078$~$\kappa$-units).
Sensor fusion (AE+UL) provides only a marginal improvement that is not confirmed by
hold-out significance testing.
The dataset is made publicly available to support standardised comparison.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================================

Inadequate lubrication is estimated to account for between 30\% and 80\% of all rolling element bearing failures~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,dai_situ_2024,nassef_impact_2022}. Despite this, lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) has received substantially less research attention than vibration-based fault detection. Accurate, online LCM would enable condition-based relubrication rather than fixed-schedule approaches that produce both under- and over-lubrication~\cite{wakiru_review_2019}.

Passive, non-invasive sensing methods---acoustic emission (AE), vibration, and passive ultrasound (US)---are attractive for industrial LCM because they require no bearing modification and can be retrofitted. AE and passive ultrasound at 38--40~kHz are both in practical use\todo{Find reference for this}, but have not been
studied simultaneously in a unified regression framework with a physics-based lubrication reference variable.

The ISO~281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$ has been used as a continuous regression target~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,jakobsen_low_2024}: a model trained to predict
$\kappa$ implicitly learns the operating-condition dependence and produces a physically interpretable output. Whether jointly exploiting AE and passive US improves $\kappa$ prediction over either alone is an open question with direct practical implications.

\subsection*{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Novel dual-channel experiment.} The first study to simultaneously acquire AE and passive US signals from a ball bearing and regress both, individually and jointly, to $\kappa$ using a common dataset and analytical ground truth.
  \item \textbf{Sub-band feature extraction for AE.} Features are computed from three frequency sub-bands (20--500~kHz, 500~kHz--1~MHz, 1--2~MHz), enabling model-driven identification of the most informative AE frequency range.
  \item \textbf{Quantified fusion test.} The first quantitative test of AE--US complementarity in a unified experimental framework.
  \item \textbf{Rigorous statistical evaluation.} Model comparisons use repeated nested cross-validation and corrected significance tests that properly account for data re-use.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
%% ============================================================================

\subsection{Lubrication regimes and the viscosity ratio $\kappa$}
\label{subsec:kappa}

The ISO~281 standard defines the viscosity ratio
\begin{equation}
  \kappa = \frac{\nuop}{\nureq(n,\, d_{pw})},
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
where $\nuop$ [\si{mm^2\,s^{-1}}] is the actual kinematic viscosity at operating
temperature and $\nureq$ is the minimum required for full EHL at speed $n$ and pitch
diameter $d_{pw}$~\cite{iso_281}:
\begin{equation}
  \nureq = \begin{cases}
    45\,000\; n^{-0.83}\; d_{pw}^{-0.5} & n < \SI{1000}{rpm}, \\
    4\,500\; n^{-0.5}\; d_{pw}^{-0.5} & n \geq \SI{1000}{rpm}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:nu1}
\end{equation}
A value of $\kappa \geq 1$ indicates adequate film formation; $\kappa < 0.5$ corresponds
to severe boundary lubrication.
Using $\kappa$ as a regression target means a trained model implicitly compensates for
operating-condition effects.

\subsection{Physical origins of AE and passive ultrasound signals}
\label{subsec:origins}

AE refers to transient elastic stress waves generated by asperity contact and
micro-fracture in rolling bearings; emission rate, amplitude, and frequency content are
all sensitive to the lubrication regime~\cite{cornel_condition_2021,miettinen_analysis_2001}.
AE sensors typically operate up to \SI{1}{MHz} and require sampling rates exceeding
\SI{500}{kHz}.

Passive ultrasound sensing at 38--40~kHz provides a complementary, lower-frequency view.
Commercial instruments compare the broadband ultrasonic level against a baseline,
triggering relubrication when the level rises by a specified number of
decibels~\cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}.
In the present experiment the UL channel uses a heterodyned probe, limiting usable
bandwidth to approximately \SI{10}{kHz}.
Whether the partial independence of the two modalities translates into complementary
$\kappa$ information is the central empirical question of this study.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
%% ============================================================================
In this section the test rig for conducting experiments of of bearing runs with varing lubrication conditions and the sensing setup are described. A single experiment was conducted at CeramicSpeed in Herning, Denmark, during which the operating conditions were varied in terms of temperature and rotational speed, such that a wide range of $\kappa$ values were covered.

\subsection{Test rig and bearing}
\label{subsec:rig}

The test rig was a custom-built bearing test stand at CeramicSpeed (Holstebro, Denmark) built for running a bearing under different operating conditions. A single deep-groove ball bearing (model SYJ25, bore \SI{25}{mm}, pitch diameter $d_{pw} = \SI{38.0}{mm}$, 9 chrome steel rolling elements, dynamic load rating \SI{14000}{N}) was driven by a VFD-controlled electric motor. Temperature was monitored by a PT100 sensor on the bearing housing.

\subsection{Sensor configuration and data acquisition}
\label{subsec:sensors}

Two sensors are acquired simultaneously:

\begin{description}
  \item[AE sensor.] A wideband piezoelectric AE sensor bonded to the bearing housing. \todo{How and where is it mounted?} Sampling rate: \SI{1.6}{MHz}; effective bandwidth verified to \SI{800}{kHz}.
  \item[Passive UL sensor.] A heterodyned passive ultrasound probe clamped to the
    bearing housing. The heterodyning process limits effective content to below
    \SI{10}{kHz}. Sampling rate: \SI{1.6}{MHz}.
\end{description}

Both channels share identical acquisition timing, ensuring co-temporal signal segments.

\subsection{Operating conditions and $\kappa$ values}
\label{subsec:ops}

The experiment follows a continuous speed-sweep protocol: the VFD ramps the shaft from
rest to approximately \SI{6000}{rpm}, with bearing temperature rising naturally from
\SI{33}{\celsius} to \SI{100}{\celsius}.
After signal quality filtering, 8\,783 sweeps per sensor channel are retained.
Table~\ref{tab:ops_summary} summarises the operating envelope and resulting $\kappa$
distribution.

The $\kappa$ value for each sweep is computed via Equations~\eqref{eq:kappa}--\eqref{eq:nu1}
using measured RPM and temperature, with $\nuop$ evaluated via the ASTM~D341 Walther
viscosity--temperature equation.
No separate film-thickness reference measurement is available; $\kappa$ is therefore
model-derived subject to the assumptions of the Hamrock--Dowson framework.

\subsection{Signal cleaning}
\label{subsec:cleaning}

Each sweep--sensor pair is subject to the following quality checks before feature
extraction: short/zero/NaN sweeps are rejected; isolated non-finite samples are
interpolated; clipping and saturation are flagged; outlier samples exceeding six standard
deviations are replaced by the local median.
After cleaning, 1598 of 1600 sweep--sensor pairs are retained.

\textbf{Frequency-domain (14):} dominant frequency, spectral mean, spectral standard

The full 26-feature set is extracted independently for each of three zero-phase
Butterworth bandpass sub-bands: 20--500~kHz, 500~kHz--1~MHz, and 1--2~MHz, yielding
$26 \times 4 = 104$~candidate AE features.

\textbf{UL channel pre-filtering.}
The UL channel is pre-filtered to 0--20~kHz before broadband feature extraction,
giving 26~candidate UL features.

\subsection{Operating-condition confounding}
\label{subsec:confound}

RPM correlates with $\kappa$ ($\rho = +0.62$) and temperature correlates with $\kappa$
($\rho = -0.68$), since both drive $\kappa$ through $\nureq$ and $\nuop$ respectively
while simultaneously affecting signal energy.
Amplitude-dominated features (RMS, variance, peak) are therefore partly acting as
indirect proxies for speed rather than direct indicators of lubrication film adequacy.
The $\kappa$ regression framework partially addresses this: training on the full
multi-condition dataset allows the model to implicitly discriminate amplitude changes
due to lubrication from those due to operating conditions.

\section{Regression Modelling and Statistical Evaluation}
\label{sec:modelling}
%% ============================================================================

\subsection{Experimental design and holdout split}
\label{subsec:modelling_design}

The dataset is divided into an 80\% training set and a 20\% hold-out test set by
regime-stratified random sampling; the hold-out set is withheld from all model
development steps.
Three regression model families are evaluated for three sensor configurations
(AE only, UL only, AE+UL):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Elastic Net} with $\alpha$ and $\ell_1$ ratio selected by
    \texttt{ElasticNetCV}.
  \item \textbf{Degree-2 Polynomial Regression} (polynomial expansion + Ridge via
    \texttt{RidgeCV}).
  \item \textbf{LightGBM} with hyperparameters tuned by Bayesian optimisation
    (Optuna TPE, 20~trials per inner fold).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Repeated nested cross-validation}
\label{subsec:nested_cv}

Model evaluation uses repeated nested five-fold cross-validation ($R = 5$ repetitions),
yielding $5 \times 5 = 25$~outer RMSE scores per model--sensor combination.
Each outer fold contains its own inner optimisation loop for LightGBM, ensuring outer
scores are not contaminated by hyperparameter selection on the outer fold.

\subsection{Statistical significance testing}
\label{subsec:sig_tests}

Pairwise significance tests are conducted at two levels.

\textbf{Level 1 --- Within-sensor comparisons (architecture differences).}
For pairs of models trained on the same sensor configuration (e.g., Elastic Net vs.\
Polynomial on AE), the corrected repeated $k$-fold $t$-test of Nadeau and
Bengio~\cite{nadeau_inference_2003} is applied to the 25~paired RMSE scores.
This test corrects the standard paired $t$-test for the positive correlation induced by
shared training data across repeated folds.
The correction factor is:
\begin{equation}
  \hat{\sigma}^2_{\mathrm{corr}} = \left(\frac{1}{n_{\text{test}}} +
  \frac{n_{\text{train}}}{n_{\text{test}}}\right) \hat{\sigma}^2,
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\sigma}^2$ is the sample variance of pairwise RMSE differences and
$n_{\text{test}}/n_{\text{train}}$ is the test/train size ratio.
Multiple within-sensor comparisons are adjusted by the Holm--Bonferroni step-down
procedure at $\alpha = 0.05$.

\textbf{Level 2 --- Cross-sensor comparisons (best per sensor configuration).}
For the best model from each sensor configuration (selected by lowest mean CV RMSE),
pairwise differences on the common hold-out set are assessed by:
\begin{itemize}
  \item The Wilcoxon signed-rank test on paired residuals (non-parametric);
  \item The modified Diebold--Mariano test~\cite{harvey_testing_1997} for equal
    predictive accuracy under squared-error loss;
  \item A bootstrap $\Delta$RMSE 95\% confidence interval (10\,000 resamples with
    replacement).
\end{itemize}
Cross-sensor $p$-values are also corrected by Holm--Bonferroni.

\textbf{Overall performance.}
LightGBM achieves the best hold-out performance across all sensor configurations
(AE-only: $R^2 = 0.951$, RMSE~$= 0.078$~$\kappa$-units, $\approx$15\% of target range).
Linear and polynomial models on AE features achieve $R^2 \approx 0.77$--$0.83$,

\textbf{Sensor comparison.}
AE features substantially outperform UL-channel features across all model families
(LightGBM AE--UL gap: $\Delta R^2 = 0.35$, Nadeau--Bengio $p < 10^{-22}$,

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================================

\subsection{Why AE outperforms the UL channel}
\label{subsec:disc_ae}

The AE sensor captures genuine wideband stress-wave emissions up to \SI{800}{kHz},
directly sensitive to asperity contact events, while the heterodyned UL channel is
limited to below \SI{10}{kHz} where the signal is dominated by low-frequency machinery
noise and VFD harmonics.
Replacing the heterodyned probe with a wideband passive acoustic sensor would be expected
to substantially improve UL-channel model performance.

\subsection{Why fusion provides only marginal gain}
\label{subsec:disc_fusion}

The UL channel retains only 6 features after selection (vs.\ 32 for AE), with peak
correlation $|\rho| \leq 0.66$ vs.\ $|\rho| = 0.89$ for AE, and its effective bandwidth
partially overlaps with the low-frequency tail of the AE signal.
Together these factors limit the independent $\kappa$ information the UL channel can
contribute.
Whether a wideband passive ultrasound sensor would provide more substantial fusion gain
remains an open question.

\subsection{Interpreting the amplitude-confounding result}
\label{subsec:disc_confound}

Amplitude features exploit speed-mediated amplitude changes to predict $\kappa$, which
is operationally useful but may limit generalisation to operating conditions outside the
training distribution.
Future feature engineering should prioritise spectral shape and ratio features (frequency
kurtosis, normalised bandwidth, spectral flatness) that are expected to carry a higher
proportion of genuine lubrication-state information.

\subsection{Implications for the $\kappa$ regression framework}
\label{subsec:disc_kappa}

A hold-out $R^2 = 0.951$ and RMSE~$= 0.078$~$\kappa$-units for a single AE sensor
represents strong performance across a demanding speed-and-temperature ramp spanning
boundary through full-film conditions, substantially exceeding prior comparable
studies~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}.
The repeated nested cross-validation design provides appropriately conservative estimates,
and corrected significance tests confirm that performance differences are assessed under
assumptions that properly account for data re-use.

\subsection{Limitations}
\label{subsec:disc_limits}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Single bearing and lubricant.} Generalisability to other bearing types,
    sizes, or lubricants is unknown.
  \item \textbf{Heterodyned UL channel.} Results should not be equated with wideband
    ultrasound.
  \item \textbf{Regime imbalance.} Boundary lubrication dominates the dataset (60\%);
    full-film conditions account for only 11\%.
  \item \textbf{VFD harmonic contamination.} Features below \SI{10}{kHz} are affected
    by switching artefacts.
  \item \textbf{Model-derived $\kappa$ reference.} No independent film-thickness
    measurement is available.
  \item \textbf{Single session file.} Temporal autocorrelation between adjacent sweeps
    may reduce effective independence between training and hold-out sets.
\end{enumerate}

%% ============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ============================================================================

This paper has presented an exploratory study of simultaneous AE and passive ultrasound
sensing regressed to $\kappa$ for non-invasive lubrication condition monitoring.
The main findings are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{AE sub-band features are strong predictors of $\kappa$.}
    LightGBM on 32 retained AE features achieves hold-out $R^2 = 0.951$,
    RMSE~$= 0.078$~$\kappa$-units; the 1--2~MHz complexity feature is the dominant and
    most consistent predictor across model types.
  \item \textbf{Sensor fusion provides only a marginal, statistically unconfirmed improvement.}
    The LightGBM combined model achieves $\Delta R^2 = +0.002$ over AE alone, not
    confirmed by hold-out significance tests.
  \item \textbf{AE strongly and significantly outperforms the UL channel}
    ($\Delta R^2 = 0.35$ for LightGBM), reflecting the fundamental bandwidth limitation
    of the heterodyned UL probe.
  \item \textbf{The $\kappa$ regression framework is validated} as a highly effective
    approach for passive non-invasive LCM.
\end{enumerate}

Future work should address the identified limitations by acquiring wideband passive
ultrasound, validating across multiple independent sessions and bearing types, and
developing physically motivated sub-band ratio features robust to operating-condition
variation.
