\section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:intro}

Rolling element bearings are ubiquitous in industrial machines with rotating elements and crucial for reduction of friction. Wear of and damage on bearings produce adverse effects such as increased friction, excessive vibration, elevated temperature, and early failure entailing replacement. This is environmentally and economically unwanted and potentially dangerous for personnel.

To extend the lifetime and reduce the friction in bearings, they are supplied with lubricant which forms a protective, smooth film between the rolling elements and bearing raceway under operation. Inadequate lubrication conditions, as such, result in increased friction, heat and wear, and are estimated to account for between 30\% and 80\% of all rolling element bearing failures \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,dai_situ_2024,nassef_impact_2022}. Lubrication condition monitoring (LCM), treating the problem of estimating lubrication conditions and acting upon them, is therefore a pertinent industrial problem. Accurate online LCM would enable condition-based relubrication when and in the quantity actually needed, rather than on fixed schedules that systematically under- and over-lubricate and without the need for interfering with the operation of the bearing \cite{wakiru_review_2019}.

Vibration analysis of ball bearings for structural fault detection and classification is mature in both research and industrial applications \cite{wang_phm_2018,shang_comprehensive_2024}. Vibration analysis is, however, less suited for LCM, as the subtle, non-stationary changes in film formation and the resulting measurable physical phenomena are masked by stronger structural-fault signals \cite{cornel_condition_2021}. More recently, data-driven work recovers lubrication state from vibration's high-frequency broadband energy, but classifies discrete states under matched conditions and degrades across operating points \cite{zhang_interpretable_2026}. Direct oil analysis overcomes the problems with vibration analysis, but in turn requires access to the lubricant, making it unsuitable for industrial applications that require retrofittable, continuous, online LCM. Conventional methods for fault detection and lubrication analysis are therefore considered insufficient for online industrial LCM. 

Sensing methods that operate online and non-invasively are well suited to industrial LCM, and among these acoustic emission (AE) and passive\footnote{Passive methods rely only on signals inherently emanating from the bearing structure in contrast to active methods, which rely on induction of energy into the bearing structure and then measuring the reaction in some way.} ultrasound (US) have shown particular promise. Both arise from the same contact-related sources --- asperity contacts, rapid pressure changes, friction, particle impacts, and crack growth --- and are therefore physically tied to the adequacy of the lubrication film, whose breakdown governs the severity of those events \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US is already in commercial use through handheld probes that flag relubrication need once a sound-level threshold is crossed \cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}. AE is more established in literature but less in practice. Yet despite each channel's demonstrated value for LCM, the two have never been acquired and characterised simultaneously, leaving their potential complementarity untested.

AE has so far been shown to be sensitive to lubrication conditions in rolling bearings through signal energy and pulse count methods \cite{miettinen_analysis_2001,yoshioka_compound_2009,hou_insitu_2025}. Compared to vibration-based methods, AE-based methods achieve earlier detection of grease contamination, micro-pitting, and general faults \cite{tandon_ae_2006,cornel_condition_2021,wang_fingerprint_2023}. Changes in the spectral distribution of AE signals have, furthermore, been tied to changes in lubrication regimes and EHL film thickness through data-driven methods, while confounding based upon the operating conditions has been noted \cite{renhart_ae_2021,marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024}. In addition, physical models connect the emission intensity and spectral content to film thickness and asperity contact rate in rolling bearings \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US, by contrast, has been explored far less for lubrication monitoring outside commercial threshold tools, with most quantitative work limited to low-cost acoustic features for lubrication-state estimation \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}.

Even with the established connection of AE and US to lubrication conditions, the literature and practical applications share limitations. The number of datasets with varying operating conditions, and different bearings and lubricants is low. Since AE and US signals are confounded with operating conditions and influenced by the transfer path, generalisation across machines and operating trajectories is difficult. Finally, while AE and US have separately been demonstrated to be informative on lubrication conditions and their complementarity argued, no study has tested this proposition \cite{shang_comprehensive_2024,wang_advanced_2024}.

Since no standard physicochemical model of lubrication condition in rolling bearings has been developed, other methods are used for estimating or determining lubrication conditions. A standard measure for ball bearings is the $\kappa$ viscosity ratio specified by the ISO 281 standard. $\kappa$ is a continuous, physically interpretable and motivated reference value calculated from operating rotational speed and temperature, lubricant viscosity, and bearing diameter, so while $\kappa$ seeks to estimate lubrication conditions, it does so directly from the operating conditions which are already established to be confounding factors for AE and US signals. Nevertheless, $\kappa$ can be useful for establishing a data-driven connection between recorded signals to a continuous regression target, seeking to quantify lubrication conditions, which is what Jakobsen et al. did with a low-cost US microphone and simple time-domain features \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}. Thus, acoustic signals can predict $\kappa$ well, but since $\kappa$ is determined completely from rotational speed and temperature (under fixed lubricant and bearing geometry), it is unclear whether the model senses the lubricant film in the bearing at all, or merely acts as an operating point soft sensor. Even so, $\kappa$ is a practical regression target which can help establish initial, data-driven conclusions about sensing methods for LCM, and the previous work begs the following questions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is AE useful for $\kappa$ regression?
    \item Do AE and passive US add complementary information in a $\kappa$ regression model?
    \item How much regressive performance is due to operating-point inference?
    \item Does this performance survive acquisition-grouped evaluation?
\end{enumerate}

The present study addresses these questions through the first simultaneous, time-synchronised acquisition of wideband acoustic emission and heterodyned passive ultrasound from a single ball bearing, under controlled variation of rotational speed and temperature, with $\kappa$ as a continuous reference. It makes three contributions. First, it performs a decomposition which separates the predictive performance of a $\kappa$ regression based upon AE and passive US into an operating-point soft-sensing share and a residual variation consistent with changes in lubrication adequacy. Second, it provides the first quantitative test of AE–US complementarity, showing that the two channels encode partly distinct components of the operating point and therefore offer complementary, rather than redundant, information about the state of the ball bearing. Third, it adopts an acquisition-grouped, leakage-aware evaluation protocol with corrected significance testing, so that closely related measurement windows cannot cross the training–evaluation boundary. Because lubricant degradation and contamination are not independently varied, the study addresses acoustic prediction of nominal ISO 281 lubrication adequacy rather than direct detection of starvation, ageing, or contamination.
