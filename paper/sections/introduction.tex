\section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:intro}
Rolling-element bearings are among the most widely used components in rotating machinery, and their reliable and efficient operation depends critically on adequate lubrication. When a bearing is in operation, the lubricant forms a thin separating film between the rolling elements and raceways, reducing friction, heat generation, and wear. When the film-forming conditions are inadequate -- because of insufficient lubricant quantity, unsuitable viscosity, contamination, or degradation -- metal-to-metal asperity contact increases, accelerating surface damage and structural fault development, and ultimately causing premature bearing failure. Lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) therefore addresses an industrially important problem: if lubrication adequacy could be assessed online during operation, and without interfering with the running of the bearing, relubrication could be performed according to the actual operating condition rather than by fixed schedules that may under- or over-lubricate \cite{wakiru_review_2019}. Developing a non-invasive method that can provide continuous information about lubrication adequacy under changing operating conditions, however, remains challenging.

The established condition-monitoring methods used in rotating machinery are primarily designed for detecting structural faults rather than assessing lubrication adequacy. Vibration analysis, the dominant approach in rotating machinery diagnostics, is effective for structural faults such as raceway spalling and rolling-element damage, where characteristic-frequency signatures can be related to bearing geometry and rotational speed, and where faults cause significant structural vibrations \cite{wang_phm_2018}. Lubrication-related changes are, however, more difficult to isolate, since they are weaker, broadband, and non-stationary, and they may be masked by structural resonances, incipient fault signatures, and operating-condition effects. Recently developed data-driven methods can recover lubrication states from high-frequency vibration, but performance is tied to operating conditions such as rotational speed --- the same factors that complicate LCM more broadly \cite{zhang_graph_2024,zhang_interpretable_2026,jiang_mechanism_2025}. Temperature monitoring, lubricant sampling, optical methods, and reflective ultrasound methods provide useful complementary information, but they are delayed, invasive, or difficult to apply continuously to a bearing in operation \cite{sun_online_2021,dai_situ_2024,shang_comprehensive_2024}. These limitations motivate the development of non-invasive, online sensing methods that are sensitive to the contact- and friction-related changes associated with variations in lubrication conditions.

Acoustic emission (AE) and passive\footnote{Passive methods use signals inherently emanating from the bearing structure in contrast to active methods, which induce energy into the bearing structure and then measure the reaction~\cite{wang_advanced_2024}.} ultrasound (US) are two non-invasive sensing methods that have shown particular promise in this setting. Both can be measured by sensors mounted externally on the bearing housing, require no access to the lubricant, and can be retrofitted to machines already in service. They arise from the same contact-related sources and so are physically tied to the state of the lubrication film \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}.

Previous studies provide evidence that both methods are sensitive to lubrication-related changes in rolling-element bearings. AE energy and pulse-count measures have been shown to track lubrication condition \cite{miettinen_analysis_2001,yoshioka_compound_2009,hou_insitu_2025}, AE responds earlier than vibration to lubricant contamination and structural faults \cite{tandon_ae_2006,cornel_condition_2021,wang_fingerprint_2023}, and changes in the frequency content and power of AE signals have been related to lubrication condition and film thickness through both data-driven methods \cite{renhart_ae_2021} and physical models \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US is already used commercially: handheld relubrication probes compare the received signal level against a baseline and indicate relubrication need once a threshold is exceeded \cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}. Beyond such threshold tools, however, passive US has received far less attention than AE, with most quantitative work limited to simple acoustic features from low-cost sensors \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}.

Despite these promising results, AE- and US-based approaches share important limitations. Acoustic signals are not determined by lubrication alone but are affected by rotational speed, load, temperature, structural transmission paths, sensor coupling, and other operating conditions \cite{renhart_ae_2021,marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024}. Available datasets also often contain limited variation in operating conditions, making it difficult to assess whether acoustic models generalise beyond the specific conditions under which they were trained. In addition, no universally accepted physically motivated or data-driven reference for instantaneous lubrication conditions exists, making it difficult to benchmark sensing data against a suitable target.

The ISO 281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$ is instead a commonly used continuous, physically motivated estimate of lubrication conditions. It is calculated from rotational speed, temperature, lubricant viscosity, and bearing geometry and is easily interpretable. However, it does not account for lubricant starvation, degradation, or contamination. Although $\kappa$ has shortcomings, it can serve as a practical indicator of lubrication conditions and as a regression target, providing an initial benchmark to show whether sensed data might be informative about lubrication conditions. In this vein, Jakobsen et al. demonstrated that simple time-domain features from a low-cost US microphone can estimate $\kappa$ \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}. To the authors' knowledge, no corresponding study of $\kappa$ estimation from AE exists. Moreover, multi-sensor approaches are regarded as a route to more robust bearing monitoring~\cite{wang_advanced_2024}, and because AE and US respond to the same contact phenomena through different physical mechanisms and at different frequency scales, simultaneously acquired signals may carry complementary information. This has not been tested, since simultaneous AE--US measurements from the same bearing have not been available.

The present study introduces, to the authors' knowledge, the first simultaneous, time-synchronised acquisition of wideband acoustic emission and passive ultrasound from a single ball bearing under controlled variation of rotational speed and temperature. Using this dataset, the paper makes two principal contributions. First, it demonstrates that fusing features extracted from AE and US signals significantly improves estimation of the ISO 281 viscosity ratio, $\kappa$, compared with either sensing modality alone. Second, correlation analysis and SHAP-based interpretation suggest that this improvement arises because AE and US encode partly different aspects of the operating conditions.

First, AE and US data are collected from a bearing test rig under varying operating conditions. Time- and frequency-domain features are extracted from frequency sub-bands of each channel and analysed with respect to $\kappa$ and the operating conditions. These features are then used for $\kappa$ regression with linear and non-linear models under an acquisition-grouped evaluation protocol that prevents leakage between training and evaluation data. Finally, SHAP-based interpretation is used to examine the contributions of the two sensing modalities. Because lubricant degradation and contamination are not independently varied, the study addresses acoustic estimation of nominal ISO 281 lubrication adequacy rather than direct detection of starvation, ageing, or contamination.
