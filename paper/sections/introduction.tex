\section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:intro}
Rolling-element bearings are among the most widely used components in rotating machinery, and their reliable and efficient operation depends critically on adequate lubrication. When a bearing is in operation, the lubricant forms a thin separating film between the rolling elements and raceways, reducing friction, heat generation, and wear. When the film-forming conditions are inadequate -- because of insufficient lubricant quantity, unsuitable viscosity, contamination, or degradation -- metal-to-metal asperity contact increases, accelerating surface damage and structural fault development, and ultimately causing premature bearing failure. Lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) therefore addresses an industrially important problem: if lubrication adequacy could be assessed online during operation, and without interfering with the running of the bearing, relubrication could be performed according to the actual operating condition rather than by fixed schedules that may under- or over-lubricate \cite{wakiru_review_2019}. Developing a non-invasive method that can provide continuous information about lubrication adequacy under changing operating conditions, however, remains challenging.

The established condition-monitoring methods used in rotating machinery are primarily designed for detecting structural faults rather than assessing lubrication adequacy. Vibration analysis, the dominant approach in rotating machinery diagnostics, is effective for structural faults such as raceway spalling and rolling-element damage, where characteristic-frequency signatures can be related to bearing geometry and rotational speed, and where faults cause significant structural vibrations \cite{wang_phm_2018}. Lubrication-related changes are, however, more difficult to isolate, since they are weaker, broadband, and non-stationary, and they may be masked by structural resonances, incipient fault signatures, and operating-condition effects. Recently developed data-driven methods can recover lubrication states from high-frequency vibration, but performance is tied to operating conditions such as rotational speed --- the same factors that complicate LCM more broadly \cite{zhang_graph_2024,zhang_interpretable_2026,jiang_mechanism_2025}. Temperature monitoring, lubricant sampling, optical methods, and reflective ultrasound methods provide useful complementary information, but they are delayed, invasive, or difficult to apply continuously to a bearing in operation \cite{sun_online_2021,dai_situ_2024,shang_comprehensive_2024}. These limitations motivate the development of non-invasive, online sensing methods that are sensitive to the contact- and friction-related changes associated with variations in lubrication conditions.

Acoustic emission (AE) and passive\footnote{Passive methods use signals inherently emanating from the bearing structure in contrast to active methods, which induce energy into the bearing structure and then measure the reaction.} ultrasound (US) are two non-invasive sensing methods that have shown particular promise in this setting. Both can be measured by sensors mounted externally on the bearing housing, require no access to the lubricant, and can be retrofitted to machines already in service. They arise from the same contact-related sources and so are physically tied to the state of the lubrication film \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Previous studies provide evidence that acoustic measurements of AE and passive US are sensitive to lubrication-related changes in rolling-element bearings \cite{miettinen_analysis_2001,jakobsen_detecting_2023}. Passive US is also already used commercially in handheld relubrication probes \cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}.

Despite these promising results, AE- and US-based approaches share important limitations. Acoustic signals are not determined by lubrication alone but are affected by rotational speed, load, temperature, structural transmission paths, sensor coupling, and other operating conditions \cite{renhart_ae_2021,marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024}. Available datasets also often contain limited variation in operating conditions, making it difficult to assess whether acoustic models generalise beyond the specific conditions under which they were trained. Acoustic measurements are commonly divided into many short segments recorded under slowly varying operating conditions. Random data splitting of such segments can allow closely related measurements from the same operating condition to appear in both training and evaluation sets, resulting in overly optimistic estimates of model performance.

Beyond how the data are split, meaningful evaluation also requires a suitable reference for lubrication adequacy. No universally accepted scalar measurement exists for the instantaneous lubrication condition of an operating rolling bearing. The ISO 281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$ is instead a commonly used indicator of lubrication regime, calculated from rotational speed, temperature, lubricant viscosity, and bearing geometry. Although physically interpretable, $\kappa$ is not an independent lubrication measurement: it is directly determined by operating conditions that also influence AE and US signals. This means a model may predict $\kappa$ accurately by acoustically inferring operating conditions. Such performance does not necessarily imply that the model extracts additional lubrication-specific information. Jakobsen et al. demonstrated that simple time-domain features from a low-cost US microphone can predict $\kappa$ \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}, but whether this performance reflects lubrication sensitivity or operating-condition inference remains unresolved. More generally, this raises the question of what information acoustic regression models actually exploit when predicting $\kappa$.

The present study addresses these questions through simultaneous, time-synchronised acquisition of wideband acoustic emission and passive ultrasound from a single ball bearing, under controlled variation of rotational speed and temperature, with $\kappa$ as a continuous reference. It makes three contributions. First, it introduces an operating-point decomposition that distinguishes between $\kappa$ prediction arising from operating-point inference and any remaining predictive contribution. This provides an experimental framework for interpreting what information acoustic $\kappa$-regression models exploit. Second, it provides the first quantitative test of AE--US complementarity, using simultaneously acquired signals from the same bearing under a common evaluation protocol. Third, it establishes an acquisition-grouped evaluation protocol that prevents closely related measurement segments from crossing the training--evaluation boundary, and reports $\kappa$-regression figures under this leakage-controlled protocol, which to our knowledge have not previously been available. Because lubricant degradation and contamination are not independently varied, the study addresses acoustic prediction of nominal ISO 281 lubrication adequacy rather than direct detection of starvation, ageing, or contamination.
