\section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:intro}
Rolling-element bearings are among the most widely used components in rotating machinery, and their reliable and efficient operation depends critically on adequate lubrication. When a bearing is in operation, the lubricant forms a thin separating film between the rolling elements and raceways, reducing friction, heat generation, and wear. When the film-forming conditions are inadequate -- because of insufficient lubricant quantity, unsuitable viscosity, contamination, or degradation -- metal-to-metal asperity contact increases, accelerating surface damage and structural fault development, and ultimately causing premature bearing failure. Lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) therefore addresses an industrially important problem: if lubrication adequacy could be assessed online during operation, and without interfering with the running of the bearing, relubrication could be performed according to the actual operating condition rather than by fixed schedules that may under- or over-lubricate \cite{wakiru_review_2019}. Developing a non-invasive method that can provide continuous information about lubrication adequacy under changing operating conditions, however, remains challenging.

The established condition-monitoring methods -- developed primarily for detecting structural faults rather than for assessing lubricant-film adequacy -- do not fully resolve this challenge. Vibration analysis, the dominant approach in rotating machinery diagnostics, is effective for structural faults such as raceway spalling and rolling-element damage, where characteristic-frequency signatures can be related to bearing geometry and rotational speed, and where faults cause significant structural vibrations \cite{wang_phm_2018}. Lubrication-related changes are, however, more difficult to isolate, since they are weaker, broadband, and non-stationary, and they may be masked by structural resonances, incipient fault signatures, and operating-condition effects. Recently developed data-driven methods can recover lubrication states from high-frequency vibration, but performance is tied to operating conditions such as rotational speed --- the same factors that complicate LCM more broadly \cite{zhang_graph_2024,zhang_interpretable_2026,jiang_mechanism_2025}. Temperature monitoring, lubricant sampling, optical methods, and reflective ultrasound methods provide useful complementary information, but they are delayed, invasive, or difficult to apply continuously to a bearing in operation \cite{sun_online_2021,dai_situ_2024,shang_comprehensive_2024}. These limitations motivate the development of non-invasive, online sensing methods that are sensitive to the contact- and friction-related changes associated with variations in lubrication conditions.

Acoustic emission (AE) and passive\footnote{Passive methods use signals inherently emanating from the bearing structure in contrast to active methods, which induce energy into the bearing structure and then measure the reaction.} ultrasound (US) are two non-invasive sensing methods that are well suited to this setting and which have shown particular promise. Both can be measured by sensors mounted externally on the bearing housing, require no access to the lubricant, and can be retrofitted to machines already in service. They arise from the same contact-related sources -- asperity contacts, rapid pressure changes, friction, particle impacts, and crack growth -- and are therefore physically tied to the state of the lubrication film \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US is already in commercial use through handheld probes that indicate relubrication need once a sound-level threshold relative to a baseline is crossed \cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}. AE has a more extensive research record but is less widely deployed in practice.

Previous studies provide evidence that acoustic measurements of AE and passive US are sensitive to lubrication-related changes in rolling-element bearings. AE has been shown to be sensitive to lubrication conditions through signal energy and pulse count methods \cite{miettinen_analysis_2001,yoshioka_compound_2009,hou_insitu_2025}. Compared to vibration, AE shows earlier sensitivity to lubricant contamination and structural faults \cite{tandon_ae_2006,cornel_condition_2021,wang_fingerprint_2023}. Changes in the frequency content and power of AE signals have been tied to changes in lubrication conditions and film thickness through data-driven methods \cite{renhart_ae_2021} and physical models \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US, by contrast, has been explored far less for lubrication monitoring outside commercial threshold tools, with most quantitative work limited to low-cost acoustic features for continuous lubrication-state estimation \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}.

Even with the established connection of AE and US to lubrication conditions, the methods share limitations: the signals are not determined by lubrication alone but also depend on rotational speed, load, temperature, structural transfer paths, and sensor coupling \cite{renhart_ae_2021,marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024}. The literature contains few datasets with varying speed, temperature, and load, making it difficult to develop AE- and US-based methods for LCM that generalise across operating conditions. Furthermore, because such signals are acquired as many short windows over slowly varying operating points, evaluation under random data splits can leak near-duplicate windows across the training--evaluation boundary and overstate performance. Finally, AE and US have separately been shown to be informative about lubrication conditions, but their complementarity has not previously been quantitatively tested.

Establishing the complementarity requires a quantitative reference for lubrication adequacy, which is itself non-trivial. No standard physicochemical model exists for lubrication conditions in rolling bearings; the most accessible surrogate is the ISO~281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$ --- a continuous, physically interpretable value calculated from rotational speed, temperature, lubricant viscosity, and bearing diameter. The complication is that $\kappa$ is a direct function of the operating conditions already identified as confounders for AE and US signals: any model that has learned to infer the operating point acoustically will predict $\kappa$ well, regardless of whether it is sensing the lubricant state. Jakobsen et al.\ showed that simple time-domain features from a low-cost US microphone do predict $\kappa$ well \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}, but whether this reflects lubricant sensing or operating-point inference remained open. $\kappa$ is nevertheless a useful regression target, providing a continuous, graded reference for systematic comparison of sensing approaches. These considerations raise the following questions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Do AE and passive US add complementary information in a $\kappa$ regression model?
    \item How much regression performance is due to operating-point inference?
    \item Does the regression performance survive acquisition-grouped evaluation?
\end{enumerate}

The present study addresses these questions through simultaneous, time-synchronised acquisition of wideband acoustic emission and passive ultrasound from a single ball bearing, under controlled variation of rotational speed and temperature, with $\kappa$ as a continuous reference. It makes three contributions. First, it provides the first quantitative test of AE--US complementarity, using simultaneously acquired signals from the same bearing under a common evaluation protocol. Second, it introduces a two-stage decomposition that quantifies how much of the $\kappa$-regression performance is attributable to operating-point inference alone, separating the operating-point soft-sensing share from any lubrication-specific residual. Third, it establishes an acquisition-grouped evaluation protocol that prevents closely related measurement windows from crossing the training--evaluation boundary, and reports $\kappa$-regression figures under this leakage-controlled protocol, which to our knowledge have not previously been available. Because lubricant degradation and contamination are not independently varied, the study addresses acoustic prediction of nominal ISO 281 lubrication adequacy rather than direct detection of starvation, ageing, or contamination.
