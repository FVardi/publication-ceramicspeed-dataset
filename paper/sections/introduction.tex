\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ============================================================================

% Importance of bearing lubrication
Rolling element bearings are among the most prevalent components in industrial
machinery, and their reliable operation depends critically on adequate lubrication.
Lubrication failure---through starvation, viscosity degradation, or contamination---increases metal-to-metal asperity contact, accelerates surface fatigue and wear, raises operating temperature, and ultimately causes premature failure. Improper lubrication is estimated to account for between 30\% and 80\% of all rolling element bearing failures~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,dai_situ_2024,nassef_impact_2022}.

% Why LCM lags behind vibration-based CM
Despite this, lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) has received far less attention
than vibration-based fault detection and remaining-useful-life estimation, which are
now mature and widely deployed~\cite{wang_phm_2018,shang_comprehensive_2024}.
The lubrication signal is harder to exploit: its acoustic and vibratory signatures are weaker than the impulsive signatures of spalling or raceway cracks and are strongly entangled with speed and load, so operating-condition effects readily mask the lubrication-dependent component~\cite{marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024,zhang_coupled_2026}. Yet accurate online LCM would enable condition-based relubrication---applying lubricant when and in the quantity actually needed, rather than on fixed schedules that systematically under- and over-lubricate~\cite{wakiru_review_2019}.

% Limitations of conventional CM for LCM
The dominant bearing-monitoring approach, vibration analysis, detects macroscopic faults such as raceway spalling and rolling-element damage through characteristic-frequency analysis, but is inherently insensitive to the subtle tribological changes of film formation and breakdown~\cite{shang_comprehensive_2024,cornel_condition_2021}. Temperature monitoring and oil analysis add complementary information but are indirect, subject to sampling delay, or require access to the lubricant reservoir, and none provides a direct, continuous, non-invasive indicator of the instantaneous lubrication regime under operating conditions~\cite{wakiru_review_2019}.

% Passive AE and US as practical sensing routes (detail deferred to Background)
Passive, non-invasive sensing, e.g. acoustic emission (AE) and passive ultrasound
(US), is practically attractive for industrial LCM because it requires no bearing
modification, no direct access to the lubricant, and can be retrofitted to bearings in service. AE captures the broadband stress waves emitted by asperity contact and microcrack events, while passive US underlies the threshold-based relubrication tools already in routine industrial use, in which the broadband ultrasonic level is compared against a baseline and relubrication is triggered once it rises by a set margin~\cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008,jakobsen_detecting_2023,jakobsen_low_2024}. The physical origins of both signals are detailed in Section~\ref{sec:background}.
Despite the practical maturity of threshold-based US and the demonstrated usefulness of AE, the two channels' signal-processing behaviour has not been characterised simultaneously within a single regression framework against a physics-grounded lubrication reference.

% Literature review part A: AE sensitivity to lubrication regime
The sensitivity of AE to lubrication regime is well established. Miettinen et
al.~\cite{miettinen_analysis_2001} showed that AE pulse count and RMS distinguish
boundary, mixed, and full-film regimes and respond to base-oil viscosity and thickener content in grease-lubricated bearings. Yoshioka and
Shimizu~\cite{yoshioka_compound_2009} combined AE and vibration monitoring on a
grease-lubricated deep-groove ball bearing using envelope demodulation and statistical AE parameters. Tandon et al.~\cite{tandon_ae_2006} found AE and shock-pulse sensors to outperform accelerometers for detecting grease contamination in a ball bearing---the scenario closest to the present study. More recently, Cornel et al.~\cite{cornel_condition_2021} showed that AE detects lubrication-sensitive surface damage such as micro-pitting and smearing up to eight hours earlier than vibration monitoring, and Hou and Zhang~\cite{hou_insitu_2025} derived amplitude- and energy-centre features that classify lubrication status across speeds and viscosity levels, though without a continuous regression target, and with no variation of temperature.

% Literature review part B: signal processing, physical models, and passive US
On the signal-processing side, Renhart et al.~\cite{renhart_ae_2021} applied octave
filter-bank decomposition with RMS--friction features to characterise AE tribological events, and Marticorena and Garc\'{i}a Peyrano~\cite{marticorena_cage_2022} showed that spectral correlation of AE resolves lubrication-regime changes under varying load, demonstrating that kinematic and acoustic features can partially decouple operating-condition confounds. The physical link between film adequacy and the AE signature has been formalised by Liu et al.~\cite{liu_combined_2021} and Zhang et al.~\cite{zhang_coupled_2026}, whose coupled vibration--AE models tie emission intensity and frequency content to EHL film thickness and the rate of asperity contact. For the passive-ultrasound channel, Jakobsen et al.~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,jakobsen_low_2024} showed that broadband acoustic features carry measurable information about the IS  O~281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$.

% Kappa as a continuous, physics-grounded target
A recurring limitation of data-driven LCM is its reliance on discrete fault labels or qualitative lubrication states rather than a physically interpretable, continuous target. The ISO~281 viscosity ratio $\kappa$ removes this limitation: it is the ratio of the lubricant's actual kinematic viscosity at operating temperature to the minimum viscosity required for full EHL film formation at the current speed and bearing geometry, mapping the instantaneous operating point to a standardised lubrication-adequacy index (Section~\ref{sec:background}). A model trained to predict $\kappa$ implicitly learns the joint dependence on speed, temperature, and lubricant properties, and its output is directly interpretable against established regime boundaries. Despite this, $\kappa$ has seldom been used as a regression objective in acoustic condition monitoring.

% Jakobsen relation and the consolidated gap
The clearest precedent is the work of Jakobsen et
al.~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,jakobsen_low_2024}, who established $\kappa$ regression for passive bearing acoustics, predicting $\kappa$ from low-cost MEMS microphone and accelerometer features using LASSO and neural networks. The present study adopts their $\kappa$ target and extends it in three respects: it adds a calibrated wideband AE channel acquired simultaneously with the passive US channel, extracts frequency sub-band features from both signals, and tests whether the two modalities are complementary. This last point is an open question: AE--US complementarity is argued on physical grounds in reviews~\cite{shang_comprehensive_2024,wang_advanced_2024} but has not been quantified experimentally, and it carries direct practical weight, since a second sensor adds cost and complexity and is justified only if it supplies non-redundant information. No prior study has simultaneously acquired AE and passive US from a single ball bearing and quantitatively assessed their joint value for $\kappa$ regression.

% Scope and generalizability
This study deliberately fixes the bearing, lubricant, and load, varying only rotational speed and temperature. Holding the mechanical configuration constant isolates the lubrication-dependent acoustic response from the bearing-to-bearing variability and degradation processes that would otherwise confound it---a prerequisite for establishing whether AE and passive US carry $\kappa$-relevant information at all. The resulting limits on generalisation across bearing types, lubricants, and load regimes are genuine, but not addressed here.

\subsection*{Contributions}

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A dual-channel LCM dataset.} The first dataset to pair calibrated AE and passive-US signals from a single ball bearing across a continuous speed (100--\SI{3000}{rpm}) and temperature (40--\SI{100}{\celsius}) sweep spanning the boundary, mixed, and full-film regimes, with an analytically derived ISO~281 $\kappa$ ground truth.

  \item \textbf{Features of sub-bands of AE signals predict $\kappa$. }
  LightGBM on AE features alone reaches hold-out $R^2 = \resHOrsqLgbAe$ (RMSE~$= \resHOrmseLgbAe$~$\kappa$-units); SHAP attribution identifies the high-frequency 1--2~MHz AE sub-band as the single most informative range for $\kappa$ prediction.
  \item \textbf{AE substantially outperforms passive (heterodyned) US.} The AE--US gap ($\Delta R^2 = \resDrsqLgbAeUs$) is confirmed significant across all tests, quantifying the value of wideband AE over a heterodyned passive-US channel.
  \item \textbf{The AE--US fusion question is answered quantitatively.} Combining the two channels yields a small but statistically robust gain ($\Delta\text{RMSE} = \resDrmseAeComb$), significant under repeated nested cross-validation ($p = \resPcvAeComb$) and confirmed on the independent hold-out set---directly addressing the open question of whether a second acoustic modality supplies non-redundant $\kappa$ information.
\end{enumerate}
