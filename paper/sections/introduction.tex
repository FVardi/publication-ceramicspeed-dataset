\section{Introduction}
% \label{sec:intro}
Rolling-element bearings are among the most widely used components in rotating machinery, and their reliable and efficient operation depends critically on adequate lubrication. When the bearing is in operation, the lubricant forms a thin separating film between the rolling elements and raceways, reducing friction, heat generation, and wear. When the film-forming conditions are inadequate -- because of insufficient lubricant quantity, unsuitable viscosity, contamination, or degradation -- metal-to-metal asperity contact increases, accelerating surface damage and structural fault development, and ultimately causes premature bearing failure. Lubrication condition monitoring (LCM) therefore addresses an industrially important problem: if lubrication adequacy could be assessed online during operation, and without interfering with the running of the bearing, relubrication could be perfomed according to the actual operating condition rather than by fixed schedules that may under- or over-lubricate. Developing a non-invasive method that can provide continuous information about lubrication adequacy under changing operating conditions, however, remains challenging.

The established condition-monitoring methods -- developed primarily for detecting structural faults rather than for assessing lubricant-film adequacy -- do not fully resolve this challenge. Vibration analysis, the dominant approach in rotating machinery diagnostics, is effective for faults such as raceway spalling and rolling-element damage, where characteristic-frequency signatures can be related to bearing geometry and rotational speed, and where faults cause significant structural vibrations. Lubrication-related changes are, however, more difficult to isolate, since they are weaker, broadband, and non-stationary, and they may be masked by strutural resonances, incipient fault signatures, and operating-condition effects. Recently developed data-driven methods can recover lubrication states from high-frequency vibration, but performance is tied to operating conditions, e.g. rotational speed, temperature, and load, which underpins the challenges of LCM. Temperature monitoring, lubricant sampling, optical methods, and reflective ultrasound methods provide useful complementary information, but they are delayed, invasive, or difficult to apply continuously to a bearing in operation. These limitations motivate the development of non-invasive, online sensing methods that are sensitive to the contact- and friction-related changes associated with variations in lubrication conditions.

Acoustic emission (AE) and passive\footnote{Passive methods use signals inherently emanating from the bearing structure in contrast to active methods, which induce energy into the bearing structure and then measure the reaction.} ultrasound (US) are two non-invasive sensing methods that are well suited to this setting and which have shown particular promise. Both can be measured by sensors mounted externally on the bearing housing, require no access to the lubricant, and can be retrofitted to machines already in service. They arise from the same contact-related sources -- asperity contacts, rapid pressure changes, friction, particle impacts, and crack growth -- and are therefore physically tied to the state of the lubrication film \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US is already in commercial use through handheld probes that indicate relubrication need once a sound-level threshold relative to a based is crossed \cite{lineham_ultrasonic_2008}. AE is more established in literature but less in practice. Yet despite each channel's demonstrated value for LCM, the two have never been acquired and characterised simultaneously in the same experiement, leaving their potential complementarity untested.

Previous studies provide substantial evidence that acoustic measurements of AE and passive US are sensitive to lubrication-related changes in rolling-element bearings. AE has been shown to be sensitive to lubrication conditions through signal energy and pulse count methods \cite{miettinen_analysis_2001,yoshioka_compound_2009,hou_insitu_2025}. Compared to vibration, AE shows earlier sensitivity to lubricant contamination and structural faults \cite{tandon_ae_2006,cornel_condition_2021,wang_fingerprint_2023}. Changes in the frequency content and power of AE signals have been tied to changes in lubrication conditions and film thickness through data-driven methods \cite{renhart_ae_2021} and physical models \cite{liu_combined_2021,zhang_coupled_2026}. Passive US, by contrast, has been explored far less for lubrication monitoring outside commercial threshold tools, with most quantitative work limited to low-cost acoustic features for lubrication-state estimation \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}.

Even with the established connection of AE and US to lubrication conditions, the methods share limitations: the signals are not determined by lubrication alone but also depend on rotational speed, load, temperature, structural transfer paths, and sensor coupling \cite{renhart_ae_2021,marticorena_cage_2022,shang_comprehensive_2024}. The literature contains few datasets with varying speed, temperature, and load, making it difficult to develop AE- and US-based methods for LCM, which generalise across operating conditions. Finally, AE and US have separately been demonstrated to be informative on lubrication conditions and their complementarity has been argued, but no study has tested this complementarity \cite{shang_comprehensive_2024,wang_advanced_2024}.

Since no standard physicochemical model of lubrication condition in rolling bearings has been developed, other methods are used for estimating or determining lubrication conditions. A standard measure for ball bearings is the $\kappa$ viscosity ratio specified by the ISO 281 standard. $\kappa$ is a continuous, physically interpretable reference value calculated from operating rotational speed and temperature, lubricant viscosity, and bearing diameter, so while $\kappa$ seeks to estimate lubrication conditions, it does so directly from the operating conditions which are already established to be confounding factors for AE and US signals. Nevertheless, $\kappa$ can be useful for establishing a data-driven connection between recorded signals to a continuous regression target, seeking to quantify lubrication conditions, which is what Jakobsen et al. did with a low-cost US microphone and simple time-domain features \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}. Thus, acoustic signals can predict $\kappa$ well, but since $\kappa$ is determined completely from rotational speed and temperature (under fixed lubricant and bearing geometry), it is unclear whether the model senses the lubricant film in the bearing at all, or merely acts as an operating point soft sensor. Even so, $\kappa$ is a practical regression target which can help establish initial, data-driven conclusions about sensing methods for LCM, and the previous work begs the following questions.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Is AE useful for $\kappa$ regression?
    \item Do AE and passive US add complementary information in a $\kappa$ regression model?
    \item How much regressive performance is due to operating-point inference?
    \item Does this performance survive acquisition-grouped evaluation?
\end{enumerate}

The present study addresses these questions through the first simultaneous, time-synchronised acquisition of wideband acoustic emission and heterodyned passive ultrasound from a single ball bearing, under controlled variation of rotational speed and temperature, with $\kappa$ as a continuous reference. It makes three contributions. First, it performs a decomposition which separates the predictive performance of a $\kappa$ regression based upon AE and passive US into an operating-point soft-sensing share and a residual variation consistent with changes in lubrication adequacy. Second, it provides the first quantitative test of AE–US complementarity, showing that the two channels encode partly distinct components of the operating point and therefore offer complementary, rather than redundant, information about the state of the ball bearing. Third, it adopts an acquisition-grouped, leakage-aware evaluation protocol with corrected significance testing, so that closely related measurement windows cannot cross the training–evaluation boundary. Because lubricant degradation and contamination are not independently varied, the study addresses acoustic prediction of nominal ISO 281 lubrication adequacy rather than direct detection of starvation, ageing, or contamination.
