\section{Regression Modelling and Statistical Evaluation}
\label{sec:modelling}
%% ============================================================================

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{subsec:modelling_design}

Three feature sets are evaluated: AE-only, US-only, and combined AE+US. Both model families are fitted to each, giving six model--sensor configurations in total. Models are trained on the full candidate feature sets (\resNcandAe{} AE and \resNcandUs{} US features); no feature selection is applied, as preliminary experiments showed no accuracy benefit from it. Interpretation is instead performed post hoc with redundancy-aware clustered SHAP.

\subsubsection{Grouping and data splits}
The segments from which features are calculated are grouped to prevent soft leakage of information: segments collected close together in time or under matching operating conditions encode near-duplicate signal characteristics and must not straddle a fold boundary. The grouping protocol consists of two steps:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item All segments recorded during the same 60\,s operating-condition hold are grouped into one acquisition-hold group.
  \item Because the staircase protocol revisits each operating point on the up- and down-sweep, and $\kappa$ is deterministic in the operating point, hold groups whose mean measured rotational speed and mean measured temperature fall within the same bin ($100$\,rpm $\times$ $1\,\si{\celsius}$) are merged into a single group (twins). Without this step, a model could score on a held-out operating point through its trained twin. 
\end{enumerate}
This yields \resNgroups{} groups of \resNwindowsPooled{} segments in total.

The groups are evaluated by a single-pass 5-fold grouped cross-validation over the full dataset. Each fold is held out exactly once, while hyperparameter selection and model fitting are repeated from scratch on the remaining four folds. Pooling the five held-out folds therefore yields one prediction for every segment from a model that never saw its group during training or tuning. All reported performance figures and significance tests are computed on these pooled held-out predictions. The procedure is visualised in Figure~\ref{fig:data_partition}.

\subsection{Model families}
\label{subsec:model_families}

Two regression model families are evaluated across the three sensor configurations,
as summarised in Table~\ref{tab:model_families}. For LightGBM, each CV fold contains
its own inner Optuna optimisation loop, ensuring that the reported fold scores are not
contaminated by hyperparameter selection on the fold test data. For Elastic Net,
\texttt{ElasticNetCV} performs an inner 4-fold grouped search over the
$\alpha$/$\ell_1$-ratio grid within each outer training partition; the selected
hyperparameters are refit on the full outer-training fold before the fold test set
is scored. The inner search is group-aware, matching the outer split structure.

\subsection{Statistical significance testing}
\label{subsec:sig_tests}

Three cross-sensor comparisons form the study's focal claims: AE vs.\ US,
Combined vs.\ AE, and Combined vs.\ US. Each is tested with a group-level
modified Diebold--Mariano test~\cite{harvey_testing_1997}. The unit of analysis
is the acquisition-hold group rather than the individual window: windows within
a 60\,s hold are near-duplicates, so a window-level test treats correlated
observations as independent and grossly overstates significance. Instead, the
squared-error differential $d_i = e_{A,i}^2 - e_{B,i}^2$ is averaged within
each holdout group, and the Harvey et al.\ small-sample correction
($\times\sqrt{(T-1)/T}$, $t_{T-1}$ reference distribution) is applied to the
resulting $T$ group means. The naive window-level $p$-value is reported
alongside each result to make the degree of inflation explicit.

\subsubsection{Overall performance}
LightGBM outperforms Elastic Net in every sensor configuration, with pooled
held-out $R^2 = \resPooledRsqLgbAe$ (AE), $\resPooledRsqLgbUs$ (US), and
$\resPooledRsqLgbComb$ (combined), against $\resPooledRsqEnetAe$,
$\resPooledRsqEnetUs$, and $\resPooledRsqEnetComb$ for Elastic Net. The size and
consistency of this gap indicate that the feature--$\kappa$ mapping is
substantially non-linear. The close agreement between the per-fold CV scores and
the pooled held-out scores indicates stable generalisation across folds.

\subsubsection{Operating-point decomposition}
\label{subsubsec:decomposition}
Because $\kappa$ is a deterministic function of the measured operating point
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}), any $\kappa$-regression performance may in principle be
obtained by inferring the operating point acoustically and applying the ISO~281
mapping. To quantify this share, a two-stage model is evaluated under the same
pooled grouped cross-validation: the features first predict RPM and temperature, and the
predictions are passed through Eqs.~\eqref{eq:kappa} and \eqref{eq:nu1} to obtain
$\kappa$. The feature-to-RPM and feature-to-temperature models are LightGBM regressors, trained per fold on the four training folds and scored on the held-out fold, exactly as the direct feature-to-$\kappa$ regression of Table~\ref{tab:models}. Table~\ref{tab:decomposition} compares the two-stage and direct
regressions.

Both channels recover the rotational speed almost perfectly
($R^2 = \resRpmRsqAe$--$\resRpmRsqComb$) and the temperature well, with US
markedly stronger on temperature ($R^2 = \resTempRsqUs$ vs.\ \resTempRsqAe{} for
AE), consistent with the conditional correlation structure of
Section~\ref{subsec:confound}. For every channel the two-stage $\kappa$ estimate
matches the direct regression to within $\approx$0.01~$R^2$: the direct models
perform no better than operating-point inference followed by the ISO~281 mapping.
The small positive AE residual is attributable to error propagation in the
two-stage temperature estimate rather than to lubrication-specific sensing. No
measurable lubrication-specific signal component beyond the operating point is
therefore detected in either channel.

\subsubsection{Sensor comparison}
Which single channel performs better depends on the model family. Under
LightGBM, AE outperforms US ($R^2 = \resPooledRsqLgbAe$ vs.\
\resPooledRsqLgbUs); under Elastic Net the ordering reverses
($R^2 = \resPooledRsqEnetAe$ vs.\ \resPooledRsqEnetUs{} in favour of US). Both
orderings are confirmed by the group-level DM test
(Table~\ref{tab:inference}). A plausible explanation is that the
temperature-mediated US structure is closer to linear and is exploited well by
Elastic Net, whereas the speed-dependent AE structure is strongly non-linear and
requires the tree ensemble; Section~\ref{subsec:disc_channels} returns to this
point. The AE--US gap under LightGBM is in any case small
($\Delta R^2 \approx 0.03$).

\subsubsection{Sensor fusion}
The combined feature set outperforms both single channels for both model
families: LightGBM reaches $R^2 = \resPooledRsqLgbComb$
(RMSE~$= \resPooledRmseLgbComb$~$\kappa$-units) against \resPooledRsqLgbAe{} for
AE alone and \resPooledRsqLgbUs{} for US alone, and Elastic Net shows the same
ordering. All four combined-vs-single contrasts are significant under the
group-level DM test (Table~\ref{tab:inference}). The absolute $R^2$ gain is
modest, but its consistency across model families and contrasts indicates that
each channel contributes non-redundant information.

Across the grid, predictions track the diagonal most closely for LightGBM---tightest
in the AE and combined configurations---whereas Elastic Net compresses the range,
over-predicting at low-to-mid $\kappa$ and bowing below the diagonal at high $\kappa$,
the linear-model signature of the non-linear feature--$\kappa$ mapping that gradient
boosting captures. Predictions fall into discrete vertical stripes, one per operating
point visited by the staircase: all segments at a given operating point share the same
$\kappa$, so each stripe's vertical scatter is the model's within-operating-point
prediction noise---the visible manifestation of the near-duplicate structure that the
acquisition-hold grouping (Section~\ref{subsec:modelling_design}) controls for. This
scatter is largest toward the sparsely sampled full-film range ($\kappa > 1$) and for
the US channel. In every panel the RPM colouring varies smoothly along the diagonal:
prediction is organised chiefly by the inferred operating point, the visual counterpart
of the operating-point decomposition of Section~\ref{subsubsec:decomposition}.

\subsubsection{Clustered SHAP feature importance}
\label{subsubsec:shap}
Because the full feature sets contain strongly correlated features, SHAP
(SHapley Additive exPlanations) values computed via TreeExplainer are aggregated
over correlation-based feature clusters, so that importance shared among
near-duplicate features is not artificially diluted (Figure~\ref{fig:shap}). For
the AE LightGBM model the dominant cluster by a large margin is the
500~kHz--1~MHz mobility/centre-frequency pair (summed mean $|\text{SHAP}| = \resShapAeTop$), followed by the 500~kHz--1~MHz
complexity--RMS cluster ($\resShapAeSecond$). The US model is led instead by a 10--20~kHz amplitude
(RMS) cluster ($\resShapUsTop$), with the 0--10~kHz complexity ($\resShapUsSecond$) and 10--20~kHz mobility features as
distinct singletons. The two channels thus rely on distinct features
whose operating-condition leanings differ---the dominant AE cluster is
speed-tracking, whereas the leading US features include the temperature-leaning
10--20~kHz mobility (Figure~\ref{fig:marg_cond})---so the SHAP structure is
consistent with the conditional correlation analysis. (The US band labels refer
to the probe's heterodyned audio baseband, not raw acoustic frequencies, so the
contrast is one of operating-condition channel rather than frequency.)
