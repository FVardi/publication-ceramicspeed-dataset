\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ============================================================================
The principal finding of this work is that combining AE and US features significantly improves $\kappa$ estimation under a leakage-controlled evaluation protocol. The remaining analyses suggest an explanation for this improvement: AE and US encode partly different aspects of the operating point, particularly with respect to temperature. The subsections below discuss these findings and the limitations of this paper.

\subsection{AE--US complementarity and channel encoding}
\label{subsec:disc_channels}

The fusion result is the central finding of the study: the combined feature
set outperforms both single channels, for both model families, in all four
group-level DM contrasts (Table~\ref{tab:inference}). The gain is modest in
magnitude but consistent, and it can be related to the
operating-condition encoding of the two channels. Conditionally on speed, US
correlates with temperature substantially better than AE
(Section~\ref{subsec:confound}), whereas both channels track speed well. The clustered SHAP results mirror this separation at the feature level, with the combined model retaining temperature-leaning US features while discarding US amplitude features whose speed information the AE features already provide (Section~\ref{subsec:shap}). The two channels are
complementary and may be so because they encode partly distinct components of the
operating point, providing a quantified answer to the complementarity question
posed in Section~\ref{sec:background}.

Which single channel is preferable proved to be model-family dependent:
Elastic Net favours US while LightGBM favours AE, both significantly. As shown
in Section~\ref{subsec:sensor_comparison}, Elastic Net recovers a substantially
larger share of the US channel's tree-model performance than of AE's. The AE
feature--$\kappa$ mapping is markedly less accessible to a linear model,
consistent with AE resolving $\kappa$ primarily through rotational speed,
whose influence on $\kappa$ is structurally non-linear
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:nu1}). Channel rankings obtained under a single model family
should therefore be treated with caution.

The comparison is further bounded by hardware. The AE sensor captures wideband
content up to \SI{1}{MHz}, dominated by verified stress-wave emission, whereas
the US probe down-mixes a narrow $\approx$38--40~kHz sensing band to an
audio-frequency baseband, within which the
10--20~kHz band carries the strongest correlations. The high-frequency
transient signature of individual contact events is largely absent from this
down-mixed envelope. Whether a wideband passive ultrasound sensor would supply
more independent information and a larger fusion gain remains an open
question.

\subsection{On the use of $\kappa$ as lubrication reference}
\label{subsec:disc_kappa}
The results indicate that the acoustic features are informative about lubrication conditions as quantified by $\kappa$. From a single externally mounted sensor, without tachometer or thermometer access, the models recover the bearing's nominal lubrication adequacy---a capability of direct value for retrofit monitoring. This claim is, however, subject to the limitations of $\kappa$ itself.

$\kappa$ is not measured but computed for each segment from the measured operating point via the ISO~281 relations and the ASTM~D341 Walther equation (Section~\ref{subsec:kappa}). It therefore inherits the assumptions of those relations, notably the use of housing temperature as a proxy for lubricant temperature and the assumption of nominally intact lubricant. A further assumption is that lubricant supply is sufficient for fully flooded conditions. As noted there, high rotational speeds can induce starvation that reduces the actual film below $\kappa$'s prediction even when the lubricant is intact. Whether the staircase protocol's higher speed steps induced starvation, and whether the acoustic signals carry a starvation signature that the regression attributes to the operating point rather than the film state, has not been investigated. The sampled $\kappa$ distribution is also concentrated in the boundary and mixed regimes; extrapolation into the full-film range ($\kappa > 1$) should be treated with caution.

More fundamentally, because $\kappa$ is deterministic in the operating point, some degree of $\kappa$-regression success is guaranteed for any feature set that is informative about speed and temperature. The conditional correlation analysis and the clustered SHAP interpretation indicate that the models indeed draw primarily on operating-point-tracking features: in performance terms, the regression acts as an acoustic operating-point soft sensor combined with the ISO~281 mapping.

In addition, since $\kappa$ encodes only lubrication changes caused by the operating point (Section~\ref{subsec:kappa}), sensitivity to degradation, contamination, or starvation is neither demonstrated nor ruled out in this study or in prior $\kappa$-estimation work such as that of Jakobsen et al. Probing lubrication information beyond the operating point requires an independent reference, e.g.\ a film-thickness measurement, electrical contact resistance, or an experiment in which the lubricant state is varied independently of the operating point.

The complementarity conclusion is unaffected by these limitations since both channels are evaluated against the same target under identical protocols. The demonstrated gain from combining them reflects genuinely non-redundant information.

\subsection{Evaluation protocol and comparability with prior work}
\label{subsec:disc_protocol}

The acquisition-grouped, twin-merged evaluation removes the near-duplicate
leakage admitted by random splits over short measurement segments, and the
group-level DM tests avoid the potential significance inflation of segment-level testing. The reported figures are consequently not directly
comparable to prior work evaluated under random splits, including that of
Jakobsen et al.; they are intended as more conservative,
deployment-representative estimates.

A caveat applies in the opposite direction as well. Because the
(rotational speed, temperature) space is densely and smoothly sampled, every held-out group
is surrounded by near-neighbour training points: the high $R^2$ reflects
\emph{interpolation} of the smooth $\kappa$(operating-point) surface, consistent
with the soft-sensor interpretation, rather than extrapolation to unseen
regimes. Same-operating-point leakage is controlled, but generalisation to
unsampled operating regimes is untested.

Finally, all results are specific to the SYJ25 bearing, Keratech~22 oil, and one 14-hour experimental session. Whether these conclusions hold for other bearing types, sizes, lubricants, or installations is unknown.
