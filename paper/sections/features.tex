\section{Feature Analysis}
\label{sec:features}
%% ============================================================================

This section characterises what the \resNcandAe~AE and \resNcandUs~US features respond to. We examine the operating-condition dependence of the features -- distinguishing marginal from conditional correlations -- and their low-dimensional structure.

\subsection{Operating-condition dependence}
\label{subsec:confound}

% Why confounding happens
The raw correlation between signal features and $\kappa$ is partially driven by the shared dependence on rotational speed and temperature. Higher rotational speed reduces the required viscosity $\nureq$ and therefore increases $\kappa$, while simultaneously increasing signal energy through more frequent contact events per second and changing the conditions under which a separating lubricant film forms. Higher temperature reduces the nominal viscosity estimate $\nuop$ (computed via the Walther equation from the measured housing temperature) and therefore reduces $\kappa$. It also reduces the actual lubricant viscosity, which may deviate from $\nuop$. As a consequence, amplitude features such as RMS are acting as indirect proxies for rotational speed rather than as direct indicators of lubrication film adequacy~\cite{hou_insitu_2025, zhang_coupled_2026, miettinen_analysis_2001}. Because $\kappa$ is fully determined by rotational speed and temperature a model given thse directly would predict $\kappa$ perfectly by construction. The contribution of the acoustic features is to recover this information without tachometer or thermometer access.

%  Marginal view
Marginally, both channels correlate with $\kappa$ through their dependence on speed and temperature, as shown in the left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond}. Rotational speed is the dominant axis for both, with energy features such as RMS positively correlated with speed, though the correlations are predominantly positive for AE and negative for US. A notable exception is the US mobility feature identified as most important by Jakobsen et al.~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}, which correlates only weakly with speed and more strongly with temperature. The marginal view is dominated by the speed sweep, however. We therefore condition on each operating variable in turn.

% Conditional view
Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond} contrasts the marginal (left) and partition-based conditional (right) Spearman correlations. In the conditional analysis, segments are grouped either into 100\,rpm staircase steps, giving the within-step correlation with temperature at held speed, or into 2\,\si{\celsius} temperature bins, giving the within-bin correlation with speed at held temperature. A partition is retained only if it holds at least 30 segments and spans at least 8 distinct levels of the variable being correlated against, so that each within-partition correlation is reliable. The temperature axis is then summarised over 22 valid rpm-step partitions (median 259 segments and 39 distinct temperatures each) and the speed axis over 31 valid temperature bins (median 232 segments and 17 distinct speed steps each). The median correlation across partitions is reported.

Conditioning reveals a clear separation between the two channels. The speed axis is essentially unchanged: the strongest AE feature (500\,kHz--1\,MHz mobility, $\rho = 0.89$) and US feature (0--10\,kHz RMS, $\rho = 0.91$) retain near-marginal conditional correlations with speed at fixed temperature, confirming that both channels genuinely track rotational speed. The temperature axis, however, diverges: AE features show moderate conditional temperature correlations (strongest: 20--500\,kHz mobility, $\rho = -0.56$), whereas US features resolve temperature substantially more strongly and with mixed sign across sub-bands (0--10\,kHz mobility $\rho = -0.79$; 10--20\,kHz mobility $\rho = +0.78$). AE and US therefore occupy partly distinct regions of the conditional correlation plane. Because $\kappa$ depends on both speed and temperature (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}), the two channels encode the operating point through partly distinct operating-condition channels: AE primarily through speed, US through both speed and temperature. These observations suggest that AE and US encode different components of the operating point. If $\kappa$ prediction primarily arises from operating-point inference, this channel separation should explain both the relative sensor performance and the benefit of sensor fusion examined in Sections~\ref{subsec:decomposition}--\ref{subsec:sensor_fusion}.

\caption{Marginal versus conditional Spearman correlation structure of the AE (circles) and US (squares) candidate features. Each point is one feature; axes are the Spearman $\rho$ with measured temperature and rotational speed. \emph{Left:} marginal correlations over all segments. \emph{Right:} partition-based conditional correlations---within-speed-step for the temperature axis and within-temperature-block for the speed axis. \subsection{Low-dimensional structure and $\kappa$ separability}
\label{subsec:pca}
Beyond these pairwise correlations, the joint structure of the features can be viewed directly. Fig.~\ref{fig:pca} shows the first two principal components of the AE, US, and combined feature sets, coloured by $\kappa$. In each, $\kappa$ varies continuously across the plane rather than forming discrete clusters, consistent with a continuous encoding of $\kappa$ in the features.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.9\textwidth]{pca_kappa_regimes.png}
\caption{First two principal components of the AE (left), US (middle), and combined (right) feature sets, coloured by $\kappa$. Variance explained is shown in parentheses. In the AE projection $\kappa$ varies smoothly and continuously across the plane, indicating a graded, continuous encoding rather than discrete operating regimes. The US projection shows the same ordering more weakly, consistent with its lower predictive power.}
\label{fig:pca}
\end{figure}
