\section{Feature Analysis}
\label{sec:features}
%% ============================================================================

This section characterises what the \resNcandAe~AE and \resNcandUs~US features respond to. We examine the operating-condition dependence of the features -- distinguishing marginal from conditional correlations -- and their low-dimensional structure.

\subsection{Operating-condition dependence}
\label{subsec:confound}

% Why confounding happens
The raw correlation between signal features and $\kappa$ is partially driven by the shared dependence on rotational speed and temperature. Higher RPM reduces the required viscosity $\nureq$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:nu1}) and therefore increases $\kappa$, while simultaneously increasing signal energy through more frequent contact events per second and changing the conditions under which a separating lubricant film forms. Higher temperature reduces the nominal viscosity estimate $\nuop$ (computed via the Walther equation from the measured housing temperature) and therefore reduces $\kappa$; it also reduces the actual in-situ lubricant viscosity, which may deviate from $\nuop$ due to thermal gradients in the contact zone, shear thinning, and lubricant ageing. As a consequence, amplitude features such as RMS are partially acting as indirect proxies for rotational speed rather than as direct indicators of lubrication film adequacy~\cite{hou_insitu_2025, zhang_coupled_2026, miettinen_analysis_2001}. Because $\kappa$ is fully determined by the operating point, a model given measured RPM and temperature directly would predict $\kappa$ perfectly by construction; the contribution of the acoustic features is to recover this information without tachometer or thermometer access.

%  Marginal view
Marginally, both channels correlate with $\kappa$ through their dependence on the operating conditions. The marginal Spearman correlations between features and operating conditions can be seen in the left part of Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond}. Rotational speed is the dominant axis for both channels, and energy features such as RMS are positively correlated with RPM for both AE and US. RPM correlations are, however, predominantly positive for AE and predominantly negative for US. Notably for AE, 500\,kHz--1\,MHz mobility is positively correlated with RPM, while 20--500\,kHz mobility is negatively correlated, indicating differing reactions of frequency content to increasing speed. Broadband AE complexity is also highly correlated with RPM. The most important US feature identified by Jakobsen et al.\ in \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}, mobility, is surprisingly weakly correlated with speed and more strongly correlated with temperature---positively for 0--10\,kHz, negatively for 10--20\,kHz. The marginal view is dominated by the speed sweep, however. We therefore condition on each operating variable in turn.

% Conditional view
Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond} contrasts the marginal (left) and partition-based conditional (right) Spearman correlations. In the conditional analysis, segments are grouped into 100\,RPM staircase steps (within-step correlation with temperature, speed held) and into 2\,\si{\celsius} bins (within-bin correlation with speed, temperature held); partitions with fewer than 30 segments or fewer than 8 distinct levels of the target variable are excluded, and the median across valid partitions is reported.

Conditioning reveals a clear separation between the two channels. The speed axis is essentially unchanged: the strongest AE feature (500\,kHz--1\,MHz mobility, $\rho = 0.89$) and US feature (0--10\,kHz RMS, $\rho = 0.91$) retain near-marginal conditional correlations with RPM at fixed temperature, confirming that both channels genuinely track rotational speed. The temperature axis, however, diverges: AE features show moderate conditional temperature correlations (strongest: 20--500\,kHz mobility, $\rho = -0.56$), whereas US features resolve temperature substantially more strongly and with mixed sign across sub-bands (0--10\,kHz mobility $\rho = -0.79$; 10--20\,kHz mobility $\rho = +0.78$). AE and US therefore occupy partly distinct regions of the conditional correlation plane. Because $\kappa$ depends on both speed and temperature (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}), the two channels encode the operating point through partly distinct operating-condition channels: AE primarily through speed, US through both speed and temperature. This axis separation provides a structural basis for their complementary prediction of $\kappa$.

\subsection{Low-dimensional structure and $\kappa$ separability}
\label{subsec:pca}
Fig.~\ref{fig:pca} shows the first two principal components from a PCA of the AE, US, and combined feature sets coloured by $\kappa$ value. In the AE projection the $\kappa$ value varies smoothly across the plane rather than forming disjoint clusters: high-$\kappa$ segments concentrate in the upper-left lobe while low-$\kappa$ segments fan out along a tail extending to large positive PC1, so the first two components---together capturing roughly two-thirds of the AE feature variance---already arrange the data along a continuous lubrication gradient. The US projection shows the same ordering more weakly: a single dense core carries a shallower $\kappa$ gradient with high $\kappa$-values mixed with medium values, and a detached low-$\kappa$ cluster sits apart from it. The combined feature set PCA, similarly, displays a smooth gradient in $\kappa$-values in the scatter plot and suggests a continuous quantification of $\kappa$ in the features.

In the AE projection $\kappa$ varies smoothly and continuously across the plane, indicating a graded, continuous encoding rather than discrete operating regimes. The US projection shows the same ordering more weakly, consistent with its lower predictive power.}
