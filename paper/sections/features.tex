\section{Feature Analysis}
\label{sec:features}
%% ============================================================================

This section first establishes that the \resNcandAe~AE and \resNcandUs~US features are informative about $\kappa$. It then examines their operating-condition dependence, distinguishing marginal from conditional correlations, which reveals potentially complementary information for inferring $\kappa$.

\subsection{Correlation with $\kappa$}
\label{subsec:feature_kappa}

% Feature-kappa correlation: regression is feasible from either channel
Feature--$\kappa$ correlation is quantified by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient $\rho$, computed at segment level. Both channels carry substantial information about $\kappa$, as shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:feature_kappa}. The strongest AE feature, the 500\,kHz--1\,MHz Hjorth mobility, correlates with $\kappa$ at $|\rho| = 0.85$, and the strongest US feature, the 10--20\,kHz RMS, at $|\rho| = 0.69$; in total, 23 of the \resNcandAe{} AE features and 10 of the \resNcandUs{} US features exceed $|\rho| = 0.4$. \todo{Wire the feature--$\kappa$ correlation numbers (and the other hand-typed $\rho$ values in this section) as macros from a new-pipeline export step.} Correlations of this strength indicate that $\kappa$ regression from either channel alone is feasible.

% ...but correlation with kappa cannot show complementarity
Correlation with $\kappa$, however, reveals nothing about complementarity. Because $\kappa$ is a deterministic function of rotational speed and temperature (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}), two features may correlate equally strongly with $\kappa$ while responding to entirely different components of the operating point or to exactly the same one. Whether AE and US are complementary is therefore a question of which operating-condition dependencies each channel exhibits, examined in the remainder of this section.

\subsection{Marginal and conditional correlation with operating conditions}
\label{subsec:confound}

%  Marginal view
The marginal correlation between a feature and an operating condition (speed or temperature) is the Spearman correlation computed over all segments, with the other operating variable left free to vary. The marginal correlation of all features with both speed and temperature can be seen in the left panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond}. Both channels correlate with $\kappa$ through their dependence on speed and temperature, but speed is the dominant axis for both, with energy features such as RMS positively correlated with speed. The correlations with speed are, however, predominantly positive for AE and negative for US. A notable case is the US mobility feature identified as most important by Jakobsen et al.~\cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023}, which correlates only weakly with speed and more strongly with temperature. Interestingly, mobility in the 20--500\,kHz and 500\,kHz--1\,MHz AE bands is negatively and positively correlated with speed, respectively. The marginal view is in general dominated by the speed sweep, however. We therefore condition on each operating variable in turn.

% Conditional view
The right panel of Fig.~\ref{fig:marg_cond} shows the partition-based conditional Spearman correlations, in contrast to the marginal correlations in the left panel. In the conditional analysis, segments are grouped either into 100\,rpm staircase steps, giving the within-step correlation with temperature at held speed, or into 2\,\si{\celsius} temperature bins, giving the within-bin correlation with speed at held temperature. A partition is retained only if it holds at least 30 segments and spans at least 8 distinct levels of the variable being correlated against, so that each within-partition correlation is reliable. The temperature axis is then summarised over 22 valid rpm-step partitions (median 259 segments and 39 distinct temperatures each) and the speed axis over 31 valid temperature bins (median 232 segments and 17 distinct speed steps each). The median correlation across partitions is reported.

Conditioning reveals a clear separation between the two channels. The speed axis is essentially unchanged: the strongest AE feature (500\,kHz--1\,MHz mobility, $\rho = 0.89$) and US feature (0--10\,kHz RMS, $\rho = 0.91$) retain near-marginal conditional correlations with speed at fixed temperature, confirming that both channels genuinely track rotational speed. The temperature axis, however, diverges: AE features show moderate conditional temperature correlations (strongest: 20--500\,kHz mobility, $\rho = -0.56$), whereas US features correlate with temperature substantially more strongly and with mixed sign across sub-bands (0--10\,kHz mobility $\rho = -0.79$; 10--20\,kHz mobility $\rho = +0.78$). AE and US therefore occupy partly distinct regions of the conditional correlation plane. Because $\kappa$ depends on both speed and temperature (Eq.~\eqref{eq:kappa}), the two channels encode the operating point through partly distinct operating-condition channels: AE primarily through speed, US through both speed and temperature. These observations suggest that AE and US encode partly different components of the operating point and hence complementary information for $\kappa$ regression; whether this translates into improved performance when the channels are combined is tested in Section~\ref{sec:modelling}.
