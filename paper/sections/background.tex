\subsection{Lubrication regimes and the viscosity ratio \texorpdfstring{$\kappa$}{\kappa}}
\label{subsec:kappa}

Ball bearings operate in one of three lubrication regimes depending on whether the lubricant film is sufficient to fully separate the rolling surfaces. In the full-film elastohydrodynamic (EHL) regime the surfaces are completely separated and asperity contact is suppressed; in the mixed regime the film is partially formed and intermittent asperity contact occurs; in the boundary regime the film is inadequate and metal-to-metal contact dominates. The ISO~281 standard viscosity ratio $\kappa$ quantifies the adequacy of the lubricant viscosity as a continuous number, which is commonly related to the lubrication regimes with boundaries at $\kappa < 0.1$ (boundary), $0.1 \leq \kappa < 0.4$ (mixed),  $0.4 \leq \kappa < 1$ (mixed, moderate), and $\kappa \geq 1$ (full film) \cite{schaeffler_lubrication_2013,skf_viscosity_ratio}. The ISO~281 viscosity ratio is defined as
\begin{equation}
  \kappa(T, n, d_{pw}) = \frac{\nuop(T)}{\nureq(n,\, d_{pw})},
  \label{eq:kappa}
\end{equation}
where $\nuop(T)$ [\si{mm^2\,s^{-1}}] is the actual kinematic viscosity of the lubricant at
operating temperature $T$, and $\nureq(n, d_{pw})$ is the minimum kinematic viscosity required for full
EHL at the bearing's rotational speed $n$ [\si{rpm}] and pitch diameter $d_{pw}$
[\si{mm}]~\cite{iso_281}. The reference viscosity is given by
\begin{equation}
  \nureq(n, d_{pw}) = \begin{cases}
    45\,000\; n^{-0.83}\; d_{pw}^{-0.5} & n < \SI{1000}{rpm}, \\
    4\,500\; n^{-0.5}\; d_{pw}^{-0.5} & n \geq \SI{1000}{rpm}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:nu1}
\end{equation}
The operating viscosity $\nuop$ is computed from the ASTM~D341 Walther viscosity--temperature equation fitted to the lubricant's \SI{40}{\celsius} and \SI{100}{\celsius} reference values, with a standard value of $\lambda=0.7$. Since $\nureq$ decreases with increasing rotational speed, $\kappa$ captures the combined effect of speed, temperature, and lubricant grade on lubrication adequacy at a given operating point. Importantly, $\kappa$ assumes nominally intact lubricant: the Walther equation reflects the properties of fresh lubricant, so $\kappa$ does not encode viscosity changes arising from lubricant degradation, contamination, or starvation. Extending the regression framework to track such changes would require either continuous viscosity updates or an alternative target variable.

\subsection{Physical origins of AE and passive US signals}
\label{subsec:origins}
AE refers to transient elastic stress waves generated by sudden energy releases in a material, where asperity contact and micro-fracture are the dominant sources in rolling bearings~\cite{renhart_ae_2021,miettinen_analysis_2001}. Under boundary and mixed lubrication, intermittent asperity contacts generate broadband stress waves that propagate through the bearing structure and are detected by piezoelectric AE sensors coupled to the outer housing~\cite{cornel_condition_2021,renhart_ae_2021}. The emission rate, amplitude, and frequency content of these events are all sensitive to the contact severity and hence to the lubrication regime, where intensity drops as the lubrication film adequacy increases~\cite{cornel_condition_2021, miettinen_analysis_2001}. Furthermore, transmission path loss is heavily dependent upon the material through which the waves propagate, and the effect is very inhomogeneous \cite{vallen_sensor_preamplifier_2021}. AE sensors for bearings respond from tens of kHz to several 100s kHz and require correspondingly high sampling rates to capture the full frequency content.

Passive ultrasound sensing detects structure-borne acoustic energy above the audible range emanating inherently from the bearing under operation. Ultrasonic emissions span from 20~kHz into the MHz range, overlapping much of the AE band. The level and characteristics of US from ball bearings change with the lubrication conditions, as the friction level, lubricant state, lubrication regime, and asperity contacts generate US signals \cite{jakobsen_detecting_2023,lineham_ultrasonic_2008}. Commercial instruments (UE Systems, Sonotec, SDT Ultrasound) exploit this by comparing the level against a reference baseline and triggering relubrication when a defined threshold is exceeded, making threshold-based passive US the most widely deployed non-invasive LCM method in routine industrial use. These probes often use heterodyned US down-mixed to the hearable domain to make human driven diagnostics possible. Such sensors use a resonance frequency around which the sensitivity is high, and which express the sensed pressures in relation to this resonance frequency. In this study a heterodyned US signal, down-mixed to <20kHz is used, but the recorded signal might even so contain energy in bands above the nominal baseband.

The two sensing methods thus respond to the same underlying phenomenon---increasing asperity contact and friction as the lubricant film degrades---but through different physical mechanisms and at different frequency scales: AE captures individual high-frequency transient events from discrete micro-fractures and asperity collisions, while passive US, after the probe heterodynes its sensing band to an audio-frequency baseband, reflects the lower-frequency bulk acoustic envelope comprising structural resonances, friction noise, and the aggregate effect of contact activity, together with energy above the baseband whose information content is assessed empirically. Both channels should therefore carry information about $\kappa$.
